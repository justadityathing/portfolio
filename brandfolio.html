BrandFolio — 5 hours. 39 screens. One investor pitch.

Both sides of an influencer marketing platform — campaign management and influencer discovery — designed in a single afternoon for a client's investor pitch. Then rejected, rebuilt around CRED's gradient language, and shipped an hour before the room.

The situation

BrandFolio is an influencer marketing platform — a client's product, not Sapmen C's own. Brands create and manage campaigns on one side, and discover and vet influencers on the other. I was brought in to replace the previous designer, briefed by the design head, and handed the client's PRD, wireframes, and design system.

What I delivered

39 screens across both modules in a five-hour window. Then a hard rejection, and a full visual rebuild around gradient surfaces — shipped one hour before the client's investor pitch. The product is live now with a revamped design language since.

The sprint

The 5-hour pitch

I had been idle at the company for a month. No work assigned, nothing on my plate. Then on a random afternoon — 12 PM — the head of design and marketing called and asked if I was free. I said yes.

Within minutes I was in a meeting with the client's stakeholders, getting briefed on BrandFolio. The PM shared the PRD and Figma file. And then the timeline landed: the client had an investor pitch that evening. The designs needed to be done in five hours.

Five hours. Thirty-nine screens. Both sides of the platform.

I didn't have time to question UX decisions in the PRD, iterate on information architecture, or push back on form structures. I followed the wireframe structure and focused entirely on making the screens look impressive — because that's what the pitch needed. The investors weren't going to click through a prototype. They were going to see screens in a deck and decide if this looked like something worth funding. So I made pretty screens. I'm not going to dress that up as something it wasn't.

The work

Find Influencers

The Find Influencers module is where brands discover creators. It starts search-first — a platform selector (Instagram, YouTube, etc.) and a search bar. The user types a name or keyword and gets results back as a two-column grid of influencer cards: photo, handle, platform, a quality rating badge, Posts/Followers/Following counts, and category tags. A bookmark icon on each card saves creators for later.

Clicking a card expands it into a full profile preview right in the results — bio, category tags, Save and View profile actions — without leaving the page. The search bar also has autocomplete: as users type, matching creators surface with their photo and name, so they can jump straight to someone specific instead of scanning a grid.

The filter panel is where the complexity lives. Brands narrow results by content category, follower range, location (country/state/city), and platform. Campaigns in specific regions with specific audience sizes need precise filtering, not just keyword search.

The work

Campaign module

The campaign dashboard is the operational hub — three stat cards (Total Campaigns, Influencers Worked With, Total Budgeting) over a data table of every campaign with ID, title, duration, start date, and color-coded status badges. Creating a campaign opens a dense form: title, description, platform, number of influencers, duration, start date, response time, plus Influencer Deliverables and Influencer Details.

Platform selection uses a multiselect dropdown — brands often run across Instagram, YouTube, and LinkedIn at once, and the form had to handle that without becoming a wall of checkboxes. Location fields use searchable dropdowns for the same reason. The final step includes a custom message field — the outreach message sent to influencers when they're invited. A real content composition step, not just a form field.

Managing a campaign

The campaign detail view is the most complex screen in the project. It packs four sections into one scrollable page: Campaign details (editable fields, category tags, follower-range badges, location chips), Shortlisted Influencers (a table of name, followers, niche, price range, status, and actions), Campaign Progress (an overall bar plus deliverables per influencer as platform-branded icons), and Individual Progress (a per-influencer table with "View Progress" drill-downs).

Drilling into an individual influencer opens a modal with a 1–5 rating scale (content quality, engagement, brand fit, professionalism) and a deliverables checklist. The rejection flow handles declines and non-responses: status updates to "Rejected" in the table, and the action column shifts from "Message" to "Resend Invite" — a clear recovery path without creating a new invitation from scratch.

Key design moment

The rejection that fixed everything

The first round didn't land. The client's feedback was direct: basic, bland, boring, unappealing. He said the designs looked like wireframes in a dark theme. He wanted something that would actually impress investors. That stung — but it was accurate. I had played it safe with flat, solid colors on a dark background. It looked functional. It didn't look like something you'd put money into.

I looked at CRED for direction — specifically how they apply gradients to backgrounds, cards, columns, even typography to make flat surfaces feel like they have depth. Solid dark backgrounds feel like wireframes. Gradient dark backgrounds feel like products. I rebuilt the visual layer with that principle. The structure stayed the same — the PRD's information architecture was right. The surface just needed to look like it belonged in a pitch deck.

Round one — flat dark

Solid dark fills. Every panel blends together. Functional, but it reads as "dark mode switched on" — a wireframe in a dark theme. "Basic, bland, boring."

Final — CRED-inspired gradients

Gradient backgrounds and accents on cards and stat columns. Same structure, now with depth through tone. Feels crafted, not default — and shipped an hour before the pitch.

The final version shipped an hour before the pitch. The structure never changed — the information architecture from the PRD was right all along. The surface was the problem, and the surface was fixable.

Reflection

The first version isn't the one that ships. Rebuilding fast is the skill that mattered.

I want to be honest about two things. First — the UX decisions here aren't mine to claim. The form structure, the information architecture, the flow between screens came from the PRD and wireframes. With five hours on the clock there was no room to question it. My contribution was the visual design layer: making it look premium enough to survive an investor pitch.

Second — the rejection taught me something MailerOne didn't. There I lost arguments about toggles; here I got told my work looked like a dressed-up wireframe. Different feedback, connected lesson: the willingness to hear "this isn't good enough" and rebuild — fast — is the skill that actually mattered. Not the gradients themselves. The product is live now with a completely different design language. My work was the version that got through the pitch. That's the job it needed to do, and it did it.
